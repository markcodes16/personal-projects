## 1. How to use the program

### The practice loop

Use this sequence: identify one recurring problem → learn the relevant lesson → practice the action in a controlled setting → apply it in real games → review whether the action happened and helped → adjust the next practice block.

Reading all of this is not the assignment. Changing repeatable behavior is the assignment. Start with the baseline, then work on one major decision skill and one short mechanical drill at a time. A useful session goal is “Before crossing into enemy space, identify the missing enemy who can punish me.” “Improve macro” is too vague to execute.

During matches, keep trying to win. Drills should guide attention without making you ignore obviously better plays. If a training constraint would sabotage a fight or objective, take the good play and record the exception afterward.

### Five distinctions to learn early

| Distinction | Practical meaning |
|---|---|
| Decision versus execution | Choosing a good fight and missing the key ability is different from perfectly executing a bad fight. |
| Outcome versus quality | A reckless dive can succeed; a sensible retreat can still end in death. Review what was knowable beforehand. |
| Income versus useful power | Souls matter through what you buy and accomplish. Farming during a decisive, reachable fight may be expensive. |
| Activity versus pressure | Shooting and moving are not automatically valuable. Pressure changes an opponent's options. |
| Knowledge versus access | Knowing an enemy has an interrupt matters only if you notice its availability before committing. |

### Useful terms

**Tempo** is a temporary opportunity to act before the opponent can respond effectively. **Priority** is the ability to move first without conceding too much, often created by wave position and health. **Conversion** means turning an advantage into a durable benefit: a structure, useful territory, a major objective, or a favorable reset. **Peel** means stopping pressure on an ally. **Weak side** is the area receiving less support. **A timing window** lasts only until the relevant advantage disappears.

### Time budget

| Weekly availability | Suggested allocation | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| 5–7 hours | About 4 hours of matches, 1 hour of review, 30–60 minutes of drills/study | Stretch one curriculum week across two calendar weeks. |
| 10–12 hours | About 7–8 hours of matches, 2 hours of review, 1–2 hours of drills/study | Use the default cycle. |
| 15–18 hours | About 11–13 hours of matches, 2–3 hours of review, 2 hours of drills/study | Add stronger practice and deeper reviews before adding more topics. |

These are planning allocations, not researched minimums for reaching a rank. Fit sessions around actual match length. Do not start a match when you cannot finish it. A typical block is a short warm-up, two focused matches, a break, and a brief review. One well-reviewed block is more useful than several tired blocks that repeat the same error.

Keep all four requested heroes in the project: Mina, Warden, Abrams, and Pocket. For each two-week training block, concentrate live practice on two of them while maintaining the other two with short drills or review. Choose the pair by your interest and current learning goal, then rotate as needed. If you want a starting allocation, give each focus hero roughly 40% of practice and each maintenance hero 10%, adjusted for queue availability. This is a suggested practice structure, not a requirement to drop any hero or a claim that one is your best main.

## 2. Baseline assessment and progression gates

### Collect a baseline before changing everything

Gather 10 consecutive recent games as an initial snapshot. If available, expand to 20–30 recent matches for broader patterns. These are practical review windows, not enough to establish every statistical conclusion. Keep patch, queue type, hero, and party context visible. A match missing from a tracker is missing data, not a loss or a zero.

Review three complete games: an ordinary loss, an ordinary win, and a close game. Avoid selecting only your best performance or an exceptional stomp. In every game, inspect the opening lane, your first two deaths, the first major rotation, a consequential fight, and the next action after a favorable fight. Review successful choices too, so you know what to repeat.

Record facts before explanations. “At 13:20 I crossed toward their camp while two enemies were unaccounted for” is a fact. “I have no map awareness” is an unhelpful global judgment. “Checking the last-known locations would have changed my route” is a testable correction.

### Skill rubric

Score each domain from 0 to 3. Use N/A when the footage does not provide a fair opportunity to assess it.

| Score | Evidence required |
|---|---|
| 0 | Repeatedly misses the relevant cue, including after review. |
| 1 | Can explain the correct action afterward; rarely recognizes it in play. |
| 2 | Usually recognizes and executes it in familiar situations; errors remain under pressure. |
| 3 | Executes across varied situations and explains when the usual rule should change. |

Domains: resource securing; movement and escape; lane trading; wave decisions; farming routes; information gathering; rotations; item adaptation; fight role; cooldown discipline; objective conversion; emotional recovery.

Do not turn the total into a predicted rank. A high score from easy games does not establish top-level performance. Preserve clips supporting each score and seek harder opposition or independent review as you improve.

### Progression gates

All thresholds below and in the lessons are **proposed training targets**, not verified Eternus norms. Increase difficulty after passing; lower the initial drill difficulty if execution is too inconsistent to practice meaningfully.

| Gate | What to demonstrate |
|---|---|
| Foundation | Complete the mechanical drill accurately on two separate days; explain your lane plan before five games. |
| Transfer | Show the trained decision in at least 8 of 10 reviewable opportunities across several games, including losses. |
| Adaptation | Explain two situations where following the rule automatically would be wrong. |
| Retention | Repeat the skill a week later without rereading the lesson. |
| Competitive transfer | Maintain the behavior against stronger opposition and when playing from behind. |

If a gate fails, identify whether recognition or execution failed. Recognition failure calls for pause-and-predict review. Execution failure calls for a simpler controlled drill. If you can execute but keep forgetting, reduce your live-game goals to one cue.

## 3. Twelve-week training cycle

Each week has a main theme, but you continue practicing earlier skills. This is the first cycle of a longer project. Finishing week 12 means reassessing and choosing the next bottleneck, not graduating automatically to Eternus.

| Week | Lessons | Deliverable | Review question |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Baseline, 1–2 | Ten-game snapshot; stable controls; two movement routes | Which mistakes are repeated rather than exceptional? |
| 2 | 3–4 | Three lane plans; trade log | What did I sacrifice to deal that damage? |
| 3 | 5–6 | Wave/reset decisions; opening-lane review | Did I create a favorable next wave or leave a problem behind? |
| 4 | 7–8 | Two farming routes; missing-enemy predictions | Did the route fit what I knew about the map? |
| 5 | 9–10 | Ten rotation evaluations; power-spike card | Was I present when my advantage mattered? |
| 6 | 11–12 | Build branches; objective preparation checklist | Did my purchases and preparation solve this game's problem? |
| 7 | 13–14 | Fight-role declarations; cover screenshots | Could my allies help when I committed? |
| 8 | 15–16 | Cooldown timeline; target-switch review | Which enemy tool actually stopped my contribution? |
| 9 | 17–18 | Conversion log; lead/deficit plans | What did the fight buy us, and what did we risk afterward? |
| 10 | 19–20 | Three matchup cards; weak-side plan | Did I adapt to observed behavior? |
| 11 | 21–22 | Communication review; reset routine | Were my calls timely, actionable, and realistic? |
| 12 | 23–24 | Capstone review; next four-week prescription | Which improvement transferred to difficult games? |

Retest weeks 1–3 skills during weeks 6 and 12. A second cycle should spend more time on your measured weaknesses, not repeat every chapter equally.

## 4. Lessons 1–6: mechanics and lane control

### Lesson 1 — Aim that secures resources

**Principle.** Mechanical accuracy must serve a game task: securing a contested resource, landing a decisive ability, or maintaining useful pressure. Aiming well at stationary targets is only a starting point. Learn the firing behavior of your actual hero and the tradeoff between attacking an opponent and being ready for the next resource contest.

**Drill.** In an available practice mode, alternate tracking a moving target with snapping to a small target. Perform three sets of 20 deliberate shots or target switches at realistic ranges. Log hits and unnecessary corrections. Then review the first five minutes of three real lanes and classify each missed resource: aim, reload, timing, positioning, distraction, or genuinely uncontestable.

**Live cue.** “What must my next shot secure?”

**Assessment.** Aim for 18 clean successes out of 20 in the controlled drill on two days, then reduce your most common controllable miss category over the next comparable block. Do not count resources you could only contest by taking an unreasonable risk.

**Advanced extension.** Practice under movement and pressure. Identify situations where attacking the enemy makes the next secure easier by forcing them off the angle. The goal is not passive last-hitting forever.

### Lesson 2 — Movement with an arrival condition

**Principle.** A route is good if you arrive on time, with useful resources, from a useful angle. Spending every movement resource to arrive slightly earlier can leave you unable to fight or escape. Learn current movement interactions in the client; timings and hero differences can change.

**Drill.** Choose two routes you actually use: lane to nearby cover and a rotation toward a likely objective. Run each five times. Record travel time, remaining stamina, exposed crossings, and failed inputs. Repeat with the requirement that you retain a viable escape action at the destination.

**Live cue.** “What happens immediately after I arrive?”

**Assessment.** Complete 8 of 10 clean routes without unintended stops and with your planned arrival resources. Check three replay deaths for movement spent before danger was recognized.

**Advanced extension.** Rehearse a second route when the obvious entrance is watched. High ground is useful when it improves angles and access; it is not automatically worth a long, late rotation.

### Lesson 3 — Melee and parry as a decision contest

**Principle.** Predictable melee becomes punishable. Your choice depends on the opponent's options, their behavior, and whether your control actually creates a safe follow-up. A combo working on a stationary target does not prove it is guaranteed against resistance, movement, latency, or a changed patch.

**Drill.** With a partner where possible, take turns defending against a randomized mix of heavy melee, light melee, gunfire, and disengagement. Use only actions the current client supports. Do 20 exchanges per role. Record premature defensive reactions, predictable attacks, and missed punish opportunities.

**Live cue.** “What response am I inviting?”

**Assessment.** Explain the opponent's defensive choice in 8 of 10 reviewed exchanges. For Abrams, test the exact charge follow-up against relevant current defensive configurations before relying on it.

**Advanced extension.** Change your pattern after showing it twice. An opponent who never parries and one who parries early should not receive identical pressure. Respect the resources you spent to create the interaction.

### Lesson 4 — Trading with a purpose

**Principle.** A trade is an exchange of health, cooldowns, ammunition, position, and farming opportunities. Winning the damage number alone can still lose the lane if it costs resources or forces your own reset. Good aggression often begins when an opponent must choose between defending resources and defending health.

**Drill.** Review 10 trades. Before watching each result, record your goal, the enemy cooldown that matters, the nearby resource opportunity, and your exit. Afterward, judge whether the trade improved the next 15–30 seconds of play. This window is a review aid, not a game rule.

**Live cue.** “What does this trade let me do next?”

**Assessment.** Enter at least 8 of 10 reviewed trades with an identifiable benefit and a feasible disengagement or justified commitment. Do not reward trading merely because the enemy lost more health.

**Advanced extension.** Practice pressure that denies access without a kill attempt. Also recognize when an all-in is better than a series of small trades because enemy recovery would erase them.

### Lesson 5 — Wave decisions and lane priority

**Principle.** Wave position determines how safely you farm, how exposed you become, and whether you can leave. Pushing, holding, and thinning have different purposes. Copying a “freeze” concept from another game without checking Deadlock's current wave behavior is not enough.

**Drill.** Pause your replay at 10 lane decisions. Predict where the next wave will meet if you clear quickly, clear slowly, or leave. Choose the action that supports a named goal: safer resources, structure pressure, a shop trip, or first movement. Resume and compare the prediction with what actually happens.

**Live cue.** “Where will the next wave be if I leave now?”

**Assessment.** Make 8 of 10 reasonable predictions and identify the cost of each departure. Wrong predictions from hidden enemy actions differ from mistakes about visible wave state.

**Advanced extension.** Coordinate a shove with your partner before moving together. If the opponent cannot be forced away, calculate whether moving still wins more elsewhere than the abandoned lane loses.

### Lesson 6 — Reset timing and losing lane gracefully

**Principle.** Staying until death is not automatically efficient. A reset has a cost, but so does remaining too weak to contest anything. When behind, preserve access to recoverable resources and avoid giving the enemy a second advantage while trying to erase the first.

**Drill.** Review five resets and five moments when you should have considered one. Record health, ammunition, spending opportunity, wave state, teammate cover, and the enemy's ability to punish the trip. Compare resetting now, after one wave, and staying.

**Live cue.** “Am I still able to do my job here?”

**Assessment.** Explain your reset timing in five consecutive reviewed lanes. Find at least one loss where you reduced further losses instead of forcing a recovery kill.

**Advanced extension.** Distinguish an unwinnable contest from an uncomfortable but playable lane. “Play safe” is incomplete: specify where you can stand, which resources you can contest, and what enemy mistake creates a return opportunity.

## 5. Lessons 7–12: economy and map decisions

### Lesson 7 — Farm routes with a destination

**Principle.** Farm the map in a sequence that leads toward your next useful action. A nearby camp may be a bad choice if it moves you away from a threatened structure or duplicates a teammate's route. Standing in useful space can also be productive without increasing your personal income.

**Drill.** Inspect five midgame minutes. Label each interval: resources, travel, purposeful pressure, preparation, combat, recovery, or avoidable indecision. Design two alternative routes and identify what each gives up. Count empty travel only when it lacks a defensible purpose.

**Live cue.** “Where should this route finish?”

**Assessment.** Reduce avoidable backtracking in the next comparable block while preserving objective readiness. Do not increase income by abandoning every responsibility.

**Advanced extension.** Assign resources by comparative advantage: who clears safely, who needs a purchase, and who must remain available? A lower-income teammate catching a dangerous wave while you take safe camps is not automatically efficient for the team.

### Lesson 8 — Information and uncertainty

**Principle.** Enemy absence expands the set of plausible threats. It does not prove that everyone is coming for you. Combine last-known location, travel time, exposed resources, objective incentives, and what the opponent can see of your team.

**Drill.** At 10 pauses before you enter contested space, list visible enemies and two plausible locations for the most dangerous missing enemy. State confidence as high, medium, or low and choose a route that respects that uncertainty. Resume the replay with only your team's information first.

**Live cue.** “Who can punish this, and when were they last seen?”

**Assessment.** In 8 of 10 crossings, identify a plausible threat and a response. Prediction accuracy matters, but a reasonable uncertainty-aware decision can still encounter the less likely outcome.

**Advanced extension.** Notice what your own appearance reveals. Showing several teammates in one place can invite an opposite-side action. Use observer information later to test hypotheses, never to retroactively demand impossible knowledge.

### Lesson 9 — Rotations and opportunity cost

**Principle.** A rotation must beat your best available alternative. Consider arrival time, local numbers, health, cooldowns, an actual target, resources conceded, and the next objective. Moving because a fight icon appeared is not enough.

**Drill.** Evaluate 10 rotations using three options: join, pressure elsewhere, or hold/farm. Write the expected benefit and likely loss before seeing the result. Include rotations that never produced a kill; forcing a retreat or saving an ally may have been valuable.

**Live cue.** “Can I arrive while this is still a good play?”

**Assessment.** Give a concrete destination, timing reason, and alternative for 8 of 10 rotations. Recognize when to abort if the target leaves or teammates disengage.

**Advanced extension.** Rotate toward future action instead of chasing completed action. Your arrival should change the local situation; it should not simply place you next to the teammate who already died.

### Lesson 10 — Power spikes and temporary advantage

**Principle.** An item or ability upgrade changes what you can attempt. The advantage can disappear when enemies shop, respawn, regroup, or regain a cooldown. Holding a purchase's value without using it can waste its strongest window.

**Drill.** Make a card for your hero's next three meaningful upgrades. For each, specify the new capability, a target interaction to test, what still defeats you, and an objective you might pressure. Validate the interaction in the current client rather than copying an old price or breakpoint.

**Live cue.** “What can we do now that we could not do before?”

**Assessment.** In five games, identify your first major spike and either use it purposefully or explain why the map denied a good opportunity.

**Advanced extension.** Compare purchased power, not just total souls. A richer enemy with unspent money is different from one who just completed the item that shuts down your engage.

### Lesson 11 — Build decisions that solve problems

**Principle.** A build is a response to your role and the enemy's tools. Your next purchase may need to improve access, survivability, sustained damage, burst, control, recovery, or farming. A popular high-win-rate item may be bought mostly in games that were already favorable.

**Drill.** Before three consequential purchases per game, complete: “I need to ___ because ___; this purchase helps by ___; I give up ___.” Construct branches for a playable lane, a pressured lane, and the dominant enemy threat. Verify present item effects and costs in the client.

**Live cue.** “What is preventing me from doing my job?”

**Assessment.** Explain 8 of 10 purchases without relying on “the guide says so.” Review whether you actually used active items when a useful opportunity occurred.

**Advanced extension.** Compare the timing of a cheaper response now with a stronger response later. Account for slots and upgrade paths. A situational item that arrives after the decisive fight solves the problem too late.

### Lesson 12 — Objective preparation

**Principle.** Objective control begins before damage starts. Check waves, local numbers, purchased power, health, major cooldowns, enemy entry routes, and the cost of leaving. The team that prepares first may force the opponent to choose among bad options.

**Drill.** Review five objective attempts from 30–60 seconds before commitment. Record who pushed waves, who watched approaches, who dealt objective damage, and who could interrupt or contest. Identify a clear abort condition. Practice the same preparation at the next live opportunity.

**Live cue.** “Are we prepared to fight here, or merely standing here?”

**Assessment.** Complete preparation or name the urgent reason to skip it in 4 of 5 attempts. Correctly abandoning a bad attempt can pass; securing an objective by luck can fail.

**Advanced extension.** Include all objectives present in the current build. Valve's June 30 notes explicitly changed the former King of the Hill event into Unstable Rift and changed its trigger, illustrating why old fixed objective scripts need verification. This guide does not assume that historical trigger remains current. [Valve update](https://forums.playdeadlock.com/threads/06-30-2026-update.146261/)

## 6. Lessons 13–18: combat and conversion

### Lesson 13 — Choose a fight role

**Principle.** Before engaging, decide whether you should initiate, follow up, protect an ally, threaten an angle, or provide sustained damage. A hero's usual identity does not settle every fight. Your current items, health, allied threats, and enemy answers determine the job.

**Drill.** Review the start of 10 fights. Write your role and the ally whose positioning makes it possible. Identify the trigger for commitment and a condition that should make you wait. Then check what you actually did.

**Live cue.** “Who starts, and what is my job?”

**Assessment.** Your first major action should support a defensible role in 8 of 10 fights. A changing fight can justify switching roles; explain the new information.

**Advanced extension.** If your highest-impact ally is repeatedly dived, test whether peeling wins more than joining the enemy backline. Compare the resulting damage window and objective control, not just your own kills.

### Lesson 14 — Positioning through cover and support

**Principle.** Good positioning lets you contribute while limiting enemy responses. Evaluate cover, range, escape, enemy approach angles, and allied line of sight. Being physically near a teammate does not guarantee that they can help through a wall or cooldown restriction.

**Drill.** Capture five fight-start screenshots. Mark your nearest cover, the largest threat, a feasible retreat, and the ally able to follow up. Find an alternative position that changes at least one of those relationships.

**Live cue.** “Can I contribute from here and survive the response?”

**Assessment.** In 8 of 10 reviewed commitments, identify usable cover or a justified reason to leave it. Track deaths caused by breaking support range separately from deaths caused by missed movement inputs.

**Advanced extension.** Create a second angle without becoming isolated. A flank needs synchronization and an exit; otherwise it is simply a long route into a local disadvantage.

### Lesson 15 — Cooldown economy and sequencing

**Principle.** Not every available ability should be spent immediately. Track the enemy tool that prevents your intended action: an interrupt, escape, defensive active, or counter-engage. Spend your resources to create a window, then preserve enough to exploit it.

**Drill.** Make a short timeline of three fights: enemy defensive tool used, your control, allied follow-up, your damage, retreat. Identify redundant overlap and resources spent too late. Practice one revised sequence in a controlled setting.

**Live cue.** “Which enemy tool must be gone before I commit?”

**Assessment.** In 8 of 10 opportunities, either track the key tool or explicitly account for not knowing whether it is ready. Do not treat a cooldown you never observed as unavailable.

**Advanced extension.** Layer control intentionally. Overlap may be correct for an urgent secure, but accidental overlap can leave the target free during the damage window. Measure the purpose, not an absolute rule against overlap.

### Lesson 16 — Target selection and useful damage

**Principle.** The best target is not always the lowest-health enemy or their richest player. Consider access, threat, defensive resources, time to disable, and the cost of reaching them. A vulnerable front target may be a better opportunity than an unreachable carry.

**Drill.** At 10 fight pauses, name your first target, your reason, and the cue that would make you switch. Review whether you kept attacking after the target became inefficient while a better opportunity opened.

**Live cue.** “Who can we meaningfully remove or displace now?”

**Assessment.** Defend your target choice in 8 of 10 cases using information available at the moment. Count forced defensive tools and interrupted pressure where relevant; do not equate all damage with equal value.

**Advanced extension.** Reposition to make a priority target accessible without abandoning all output. Learn when threatening them is enough to deny their contribution while your team wins elsewhere.

### Lesson 17 — Convert fights into progress

**Principle.** A won fight creates a limited action window. Reassess waves, health, resources, enemy respawns, available structures, and objective risk immediately. Chasing an extra kill can spend the entire window while the durable reward disappears.

**Drill.** Review five favorable fights. Pause when the immediate danger ends and choose among structure, major objective, resource denial, wave improvement, or reset. State the latest safe departure condition. Compare with the next minute of actual play.

**Live cue.** “What did this fight buy us?”

**Assessment.** Make a deliberate follow-up choice in 4 of 5 favorable situations. Do not penalize a reset when the team lacks the resources to continue safely.

**Advanced extension.** Compare a high-certainty small gain with a larger contestable gain. The team does not need to attempt the largest objective on the map after every pick.

### Lesson 18 — Play from ahead and behind

**Principle.** When ahead, constrain the opponent's options without donating isolated deaths. When behind, protect useful resources, avoid repeated disadvantaged entries, and identify a concentrated opportunity. “Always avoid fights when behind” fails when waiting guarantees a worse position.

**Drill.** Select one lead and one deficit replay. At three timestamps in each, write the safest useful action and the more ambitious alternative. Describe what information would justify the risk. Evaluate team resources and local numbers separately.

**Live cue.** “What risk does our position justify?”

**Assessment.** Show one controlled conversion while ahead and one planned recovery attempt while behind. Both require a reason beyond confidence or frustration.

**Advanced extension.** Distinguish total team disadvantage from a favorable local matchup. An enemy showing far away may create a brief opportunity even while your team's overall economy is worse.

## 7. Lessons 19–24: advanced adaptation

### Lesson 19 — Matchup preparation

**Principle.** A matchup is a set of interactions at specific ranges, timings, and resource states. “This hero counters me” hides the actual problem. Determine which tool makes your default action fail and how to change the interaction.

**Drill.** Build cards for three frequent or difficult opponents. Include their punish tool, your safe resource angle, your aggression trigger, reset needs, and the purchase or sequencing change to test. Use current tooltips and replay evidence.

**Live cue.** “What interaction am I trying to avoid or create?”

**Assessment.** Explain and test one actionable adaptation per card. Revisit after several examples; one unusual opponent does not establish the matchup.

**Advanced extension.** Separate hero matchup from player habit. If an opponent wastes their escape repeatedly, exploit that behavior without assuming every player of that hero will do the same.

### Lesson 20 — Weak-side play and resource allocation

**Principle.** You cannot receive full team support everywhere. When teammates invest elsewhere, choose which resources you can keep and which pressure you can safely apply. Dying to defend an already lost structure can turn a local concession into a map-wide loss.

**Drill.** Review five periods when your team grouped away from you. Identify enemies who could reach you, the deepest safe position supported by available information, and the cue requiring retreat. Compare your personal reward with the team cost if you died.

**Live cue.** “What can I safely hold while the team acts elsewhere?”

**Assessment.** Make a defensible hold, retreat, or cross-map trade in 4 of 5 opportunities. Safety does not mean disappearing from all useful space.

**Advanced extension.** Coordinate who catches which wave before an objective. A durable hero is not automatically the correct collector if their absence removes your team's only reliable engage or peel.

### Lesson 21 — Communication that changes action

**Principle.** Useful communication is early, brief, and actionable. State location, relevant resource, and intended action. “Two missing toward our side; back after this wave” gives teammates more usable information than “be careful.”

**Drill.** Review five calls or pings. Ask whether the information was available, whether it arrived before the decision, and whether the requested action was feasible. Rewrite vague calls into one sentence. If communication was not recorded, mark the evidence missing.

**Live cue.** “What does my teammate need to do differently?”

**Assessment.** Produce five timely calls in a session without narrating every thought. An unheeded good call can still pass; repeated calls after teammates cannot respond add little.

**Advanced extension.** Call a fallback. “We can pressure this if they stay visible; otherwise reset” establishes a shared boundary. Adapt to what teammates actually do instead of sacrificing yourself to prove the original call was right.

### Lesson 22 — Tilt recovery and attention

**Principle.** Competitive frustration matters when it changes decisions: rushed re-entry, revenge chasing, careless shopping, or abandoning information checks. Work with observable behaviors rather than judging your character or pretending you should never feel annoyed.

**Drill.** Before a session, rate focus and frustration from 1–5 for your own tracking. After a difficult death, use a brief reset: release tension, identify the next useful task, and name one fact about the map. Afterward, log whether the next decision repeated the same mistake.

**Live cue.** “What is the next useful action?”

**Assessment.** Recover the routine after three frustrating events. If attention remains poor across two games, stop queuing and switch to a short review or finish the session. Loss count alone is not the trigger.

**Advanced extension.** Separate accountability from blame. “I committed before follow-up was possible” is actionable even when a teammate also erred. Review team errors only insofar as they change your future decisions.

### Lesson 23 — Patch adaptation and hypothesis testing

**Principle.** Strong players update their beliefs. After a patch, ask which previously good action changes, why, and how you will test it. A list of numerical changes is less useful than understanding their effect on a lane, purchase, or fight window.

**Drill.** Choose three changes affecting your hero, frequent opponent, or objective. Write: old assumption; new information; predicted consequence; controlled test; live-game evidence needed. Mark untested assumptions explicitly.

**Live cue.** “Am I relying on an interaction I have not rechecked?”

**Assessment.** Update one matchup card, one build branch, and one timing note when relevant changes occur. A justified decision to retain a build is a valid outcome.

**Advanced extension.** Compare contemporary games with similar conditions. Do not conclude that an item change caused a win-rate movement without considering who bought it, when, and the sample size.

### Lesson 24 — Stronger opposition and organized play

**Principle.** A tactic that succeeds because opponents do not punish it may fail at the next level. Organized opponents can coordinate responses that are rare in ordinary matchmaking. Test your habits under better resistance while continuing to develop individual consistency.

**Drill.** Use available custom games, practice partners, or suitable organized matches. Choose one team concept: synchronized entry, objective preparation, protecting a damage dealer, or resource assignments. Define roles before play and review whether each person could execute their part.

**Live cue.** “What would a prepared opponent do to this plan?”

**Assessment.** Complete a capstone of five consecutive reviewed games, including losses. Provide one successful adaptation, one failed decision, one execution error, and the next practice prescription with clips or timestamps.

**Advanced extension.** Add draft and composition study if the competition format uses drafting. Explain engage, disengage, damage access, wave control, scaling, and objective threats for both teams. Verify the actual event's format before preparing bans or picks.

## 8. Hero specializations

These are coaching applications, not claims that a particular current build is optimal. Ability names were checked against the linked hero pages; detailed interactions and upgrade breakpoints still need in-client verification. Choose a main after baseline review.

### Mina — Judge the commitment before chasing the finish

The current reference lists Rake, Sanguine Retreat, Love Bites, and Nox Nostra. Its Rake description ties damage to the target's missing health. Verify current ability details in the client before practicing exact sequences. [Mina overview](https://tracklock.gg/heroes/mina/overview)

**Training focus:** Recognize a genuinely punishable target, approach with a plan, and avoid turning an apparent finishing opportunity into an unsupported death. This is a proposed training emphasis, not a diagnosis of your Mina play or a prescription for one mandatory build.

**Drill A — Finishing judgment.** In practice, test Rake against targets at several health levels using the same items and relevant defensive settings. Record when the attack is sufficient and when follow-up is needed. Repeat with a different defensive configuration. Learn a reliable estimate for the tested setup; do not generalize one result into a universal execute threshold.

**Drill B — Commitment and retreat.** Read and test Sanguine Retreat's current activation, timing, and restrictions. Rehearse three situations: approaching a target, leaving after applying pressure, and abandoning an attempted finish when danger appears. For each, name the cover or allied support you want to reach. If the current ability does not support the response you expected, change the sequence instead of forcing it.

**Drill C — Target access.** Review 10 opportunities to attack a weakened enemy. Before revealing the outcome, check their defensive tools, nearby teammates, your available resources, and the reward beyond the kill. Score choosing not to chase as a success when the route was unjustifiable. Also identify opportunities you passed up despite a favorable setup.

**Drill D — Major ability purpose.** Before using Nox Nostra, name the immediate job and how teammates can capitalize under its current rules. Review whether the use changed the fight or simply prolonged an already poor commitment. Test Love Bites within your current sequence and record what it contributes rather than inheriting assumptions from an older build.

**Live cue:** “Is this target actually finishable, and what happens after I go in?”

**Promotion evidence:** Give a sound commit-or-abort explanation for 8 of 10 reviewed opportunities and reproduce your tested sequence on two separate days. Do not reward fewer deaths if they came from refusing every useful risk.

**Lesson emphasis:** 3–4, 8, 10–11, and 13–17. Pair target-access review with farming and rotation review so chase decisions are evaluated against what you could have gained elsewhere.

### Warden — Turn pressure into a reachable commitment

The current reference lists Alchemical Flask, Willpower, Binding Word, and Last Stand. [Warden overview](https://tracklock.gg/heroes/warden/overview)

**Training focus:** Create situations where your control and close pressure can matter without spending all resources just to enter. Your replay question is whether the target was realistically punishable, not simply whether you pressed your abilities.

Practice three situations: pressuring someone near cover, following allied control, and protecting a teammate from a diver. Before each commitment, name the escape or interrupt that can stop the play. Evaluate whether Binding Word creates meaningful pressure even when it does not lead to a kill; displacement may free a wave or objective. Verify current behavior rather than assuming an old sequence remains optimal.

For Last Stand, review the setup, approach, and enemy responses. Classify failed uses by cause: poor timing, unreachable targets, interruption, insufficient durability, or unsupported commitment. These causes demand different fixes. More damage cannot solve every access problem.

**Mini-program:** In a controlled setting, repeat your intended approach and follow-up 10 times from different starting distances. In five real games, log your first two major commitments and the next team benefit. Compare gun-focused pressure and a more initiation-oriented role only after defining what each must accomplish.

**Promotion evidence:** Your engage is reachable, your allies can act, and you can explain when to hold your commitment. Do not judge mastery by ultimate damage alone.

### Abrams — Control space without becoming predictable

The current reference lists Siphon Life, Shoulder Charge, Infernal Resilience, and Seismic Impact. [Abrams overview](https://tracklock.gg/heroes/abrams/overview)

**Training focus:** Choose useful close-range interactions, manage counterplay, and connect your durability to a team purpose. Living a long time while your team cannot capitalize is not automatically effective frontlining.

Practice charge geometry from several common positions and terrain angles in the current map. Record where the target ends up and whether your allies can follow. Rehearse the decision after contact: gunfire, melee if genuinely safe, control follow-up, or disengagement. Test relevant defensive setups instead of treating charge into heavy melee as universally guaranteed.

In fights, choose explicitly between access to an enemy and protection of an ally. If your damage dealer wins when left free, using your control defensively may produce more value than chasing a second target. Watch whether anti-recovery tools or concentrated damage change how long you can remain exposed; verify present item effects before choosing the response.

**Mini-program:** Ten geometry repetitions, 20 mixed melee/parry exchanges, and five replay reviews comparing engage versus peel. Record predictable heavy attacks, unsupported entries, and useful space created.

**Promotion evidence:** You vary attacks against the opponent's behavior, reserve or spend control deliberately, and survive or trade for a concrete team benefit. Death count alone does not distinguish a valuable sacrifice from an avoidable donation.

### Pocket — Plan the entry, sequence, and exit together

The current reference lists Barrage, Flying Cloak, Enchanter's Satchel, and Affliction. [Pocket overview](https://tracklock.gg/heroes/pocket/overview)

**Training focus:** Apply meaningful fight pressure while retaining a deliberate survival plan. Learn the current interactions among your damage, repositioning, and defensive tools before relying on an old combo. The goal is to make repeated useful contributions, not merely complete a fast input sequence.

**Drill A — Two commitment levels.** In the current practice environment, rehearse one sequence that preserves a repositioning or defensive option and one full commitment. Run each 10 times from varied starting positions. Name the threat that the reserved tool is intended to answer. Test current cloak and satchel behavior directly; do not assume every control effect permits the same response.

**Drill B — Fight entry.** Review five engagements from before you move in. Identify allied follow-up, the enemy control that matters, your intended target area, and your exit destination. Classify the failure as a poor decision, a missed input, or changed information. A polished combo into an unsupported position is still a decision error.

**Drill C — Affliction follow-through.** Review five uses and ask what pressure they created: did enemies lose access to a position, spend a defensive resource, retreat, or become easier for allies to finish? Record whether your team could exploit that pressure. Check the current ability's damage and removal rules before drawing conclusions about an individual target surviving.

**Live cue:** “What gets me out, and what enemy tool prevents it?”

**Promotion evidence:** Execute 8 of 10 controlled sequences cleanly on two days, then show a justified entry and response plan in 8 of 10 reviewed opportunities. These are training targets, not Eternus benchmarks. A deliberate sacrifice for a decisive team benefit can be correct; an impressive damage total alone does not establish that benefit.

**Lesson emphasis:** 2, 8, 13–16, and 17. Keep resource securing and lane control in the schedule so mechanical practice does not conceal an economy problem.

Each branch should contain the problem, present item options, timing, opportunity cost, and interaction test. Use a current specialist build as an initial hypothesis, then learn why its choices exist. ## 9. Replay analysis and learning from strong players

### A 15-minute review

Spend two minutes locating a consequential event, five minutes reviewing the setup, five minutes comparing alternatives, and three minutes writing the next training cue. Expand when the decision needs more context; do not spend the entire review repeatedly watching the death animation.

Start 20–40 seconds before a death or commitment. Ask what earlier decision made the outcome likely. A death can originate in a missed reset, a bad route, unnecessary stamina expenditure, or a failure to notice an enemy disappearance long before the final mechanical error.

For each event record: available information; intended goal; chosen action; best realistic alternative; result; decision quality; execution quality; confidence; next drill. Grade decision and execution separately as good, mixed, or poor. “Unclear” is better than inventing missing context.

### Correcting hindsight bias

First watch from your perspective and team-visible information. Write a prediction before revealing the outcome. Only then use broader observer information to understand what happened. Do not classify a play as obviously bad merely because an unseen enemy happened to be there.

Evaluate the alternative's cost. “I should have stayed safe” is incomplete if staying safe would concede a decisive objective. Conversely, “we needed to do something” does not justify arriving alone into an already lost fight.

### How to watch a strong player

Prefer a recent full match of the same hero, a comparable role, and a reasonably similar game state. Include difficult lanes and losses. Highlights conceal downtime, failed plans, and recovery decisions.

Pause before a shop, rotation, engage, or objective decision. Predict the next action and explain yours. Watch what the player does. Record the difference and a possible reason. A disagreement becomes a research question; their action is not automatically correct because of their rank.

Extract one transferable rule, one exception, and one drill from each viewing session. Save a timestamp and the observed game context. If your practice time is limited, finish this extraction before starting another video.

### Verified data routes

### Evidence levels

| Input | What it can support | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Profile and match summaries | Hero usage, results, duration, available aggregate statistics | Why you died, quality of an engage, missed opportunities |
| Detailed match timeline, if available | Timing of purchases, economy changes, deaths, objective events | Your intent, complete vision, communication quality |
| POV recording | Positioning, visible cues, sequencing, aim, camera decisions | Exact private teammate intent or information outside the recording |
| Your notes and team communication | Intended plan, fatigue, calls, perceived constraints | An objective explanation without checking the actual play |

A good analysis joins these layers. Statistics suggest where to inspect; footage tests the explanation.

### Analysis workflow after you provide a profile

1. Verify the account and data coverage. Check a few recent in-client matches against the retrieved list.
2. Create a baseline grouped by patch, hero, and queue. Preserve missing fields and collection dates.
3. Rank candidate problems by recurrence and consequence. Avoid diagnosing everything at once.
4. Select representative replay moments that could confirm or refute each candidate.
5. Choose one intervention and define its metric before the next block.
6. Reassess after a comparable set of opportunities. Keep, modify, or replace the intervention.

The resulting personal report should contain: data coverage; hero-by-hero baseline; three prioritized bottlenecks; timestamps supporting them; the next two-week lesson plan; and a clear account of uncertainty. None of those personal findings should be invented from your hero choices.

### Metric definitions

These are proposed coaching measurements. Some require manual replay annotation and may not be available in a tracker.

| Metric | Definition | Interpretation guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Win rate | Wins / completed reviewed matches | Show the count and time window; short runs are noisy. |
| Souls per minute | Match souls / match duration in minutes, using a consistent source definition | Compare similar heroes and game contexts; extra farming is not always useful. |
| Economy at 5/10 minutes | Observed souls at those timestamps, where available | Do not infer from end-game totals; account for lane context. |
| Controllable resource misses | Misses attributable to execution or positioning / reviewable opportunities | Mark uncontestable resources separately. |
| Avoidable pre-fight deaths | Reviewed avoidable deaths that remove you from a relevant upcoming contest | Requires a defined contest and a fair alternative; no arbitrary automatic classification. |
| Useful readiness | Relevant planned fights you reached with a usable role and resources / such fights reviewed | Do not punish a justified cross-map action as mere absence. |
| Active-item use | Useful activations / clear safe opportunities to activate | Requires footage; using an item on cooldown is not the goal. |
| Conversion decisions | Favorable situations followed by a justified action / favorable situations reviewed | A reset can qualify; a lucky objective take can still be poor. |
| Repeated error count | Instances of the same precisely defined error per review window | Include the number of opportunities so fewer games do not fake improvement. |

Useful contextual metrics include damage, healing, objective damage, kill participation, and deaths, where available. None should become a solo performance grade. Damage can be irrelevant poke; low deaths can reflect excessive passivity; high kill participation can coexist with abandoned waves.

### Worked diagnostic example — fictional

Suppose 20 games show lower-than-your-usual midgame income. The initial hypothesis is poor farming routes. Review then finds that in six of eight inspected intervals you were walking toward fights that ended before arrival. The more specific problem is late rotation commitment.

The prescription becomes lesson 9, not “farm more”: before leaving, estimate arrival usefulness and name the alternative. The next block tracks avoidable late arrivals per rotation opportunity, plus whether you still attend reachable important fights. If late arrivals fall while useful presence rises, the intervention has promising evidence even before rank changes. These numbers are illustrative, not your actual results.

### Comparisons and sample size

Compare yourself with your own previous comparable block first. For elite references, match hero, patch, queue, game duration, and role as well as the available data permits. Show how many matches remain after filtering. Small samples should produce hypotheses, not confident rankings.

A 12–8 record in 20 games is 60%, but an approximate 95% Wilson interval spans roughly 39%–78%. That wide range illustrates why one short run should not decide your main hero or prove a training effect. Games also share teammates, patches, and learning trends, so even a basic interval does not capture every dependency.

Use medians and spread for skewed performance measures; retain unusual games with context instead of deleting losses that hurt the story. Pre/post improvement is not automatically caused by the drill: opponents, hero strength, and party composition may have changed.

## 11. Scenario exam

Answer before reading the key. For each scenario, give an action, two supporting facts, one uncertainty, and a condition that changes your decision. These are open-response decision exercises, not a quiz that assumes one action is correct regardless of context.

### Scenarios

1. You can damage your lane opponent, but your ammunition is nearly empty and two resources will become contestable shortly. Your opponent still has an escape available. What is your next priority?
2. You can reach a nearby fight in about 12 seconds. Your teammate is already low, the enemy has local numbers, and a wave is approaching your structure. Do you rotate?
3. Your team wins a fight with two allies low and several major abilities spent. A nearby structure is accessible; a larger neutral objective may be contested by surviving enemies. What do you propose?
4. You are ahead on Abrams. The enemy's high-impact diver keeps reaching your strongest damage dealer while you chase their support. What role change should you test?
5. You are playing Warden and repeatedly fail to make your major commitment matter because targets escape your approach. Is more damage necessarily the next purchase?
6. On Pocket, you can commit your remaining repositioning tool to reach a weakened enemy. Your defensive option is unavailable, and an enemy control tool has not been seen used. What must you check before committing?
7. A missing enemy could be near your next camp or preparing an objective. You have no fresh confirmation. How do you choose a route?
8. You lose three games while consistently executing the session goal and remaining focused. Must you stop? How would the answer change if you were revenge chasing?
9. A late-game item has a much higher displayed win rate than a cheaper alternative. Does that establish it as the correct next buy?
10. Your team is behind overall, but two enemies are visibly far away and the remaining group is exposed near your strongest teammates. Must you avoid the fight?
11. A mechanic you learned in an older guide no longer behaves consistently in practice. What changes in the study plan?
12. You died to an enemy only revealed by observer view. The route looked reasonable using team-visible information. How do you grade the decision?

### Answer key and reasoning

1. Usually preserve the ability to contest the resources and avoid starting a low-conversion trade. Reload timing and cover may decide the exact action. Change if your pressure can safely force the opponent completely off the resources or secure a highly favorable kill.
2. Usually hold or take a different useful action if arrival is too late to change the fight. Check whether a retreat path, unusually strong arrival, or a strategically necessary defense changes that assessment. Do not add a delayed death to the first one.
3. Prefer the reward your available resources can reliably secure, potentially the structure or a reset. The larger objective needs an assessment of remaining enemies and ability to contest. “We won a fight” does not mean every follow-up is safe.
4. Test peeling or counter-engaging to preserve your damage dealer's output. Compare the effect with your original dive. Change if another ally can already protect them or your own access reliably wins the fight faster.
5. No. Diagnose access, timing, enemy escape, control, and support first. A mobility or control response, changed approach, or waiting for an enemy tool may be more relevant than additional damage.
6. Check whether the target is actually finishable, who can control you, whether allies can follow up, and what happens after the commitment. An available reposition does not guarantee a safe exit. Preserve it or choose a less committed action if the likely response makes the finish a poor trade; a decisive, supported opportunity could justify spending it.
7. Choose a route with acceptable downside across plausible enemy locations; retain an exit and seek information through safe positioning. Do not pretend the missing enemy is definitely elsewhere or definitely waiting for you.
8. Not necessarily. Outcomes alone do not measure focus. Continue only if you have time and attention. Repeated revenge chasing is evidence to pause, review, or end the block because execution of the learning process has broken down.
9. No. Purchase timing, cost, selection effects, hero/role differences, and game state can explain the association. Ask which item solves the current obstacle before the next consequential action.
10. No. Evaluate the local window, enemy return time, your resources, and the reward. A concentrated favorable opportunity can be the correct recovery play. Abort if new information removes the advantage.
11. Mark the old assumption unverified, inspect current client behavior and patch information, and rebuild the drill around a tested interaction. Keep the underlying lesson if it still applies.
12. Judge the information-based risk fairly. Check whether earlier visible cues were missed, but do not demand observer knowledge. A reasonable decision with a bad result can remain reasonable.

**Scoring:** Award one point for a defensible action, one for relevant evidence, one for acknowledging uncertainty, and one for a sensible switch condition: 48 possible points. Use 38/48 as an initial study target, not a rank predictor. Revisit any answer that would require information you did not possess.

### Additions worth making

**Personal dashboard after the baseline.** Track opportunities, repeated errors, and a small set of relevant outcomes. Start manually. Automating unclear metrics produces confident-looking noise. A dashboard should help choose the next lesson, not overwhelm you with every available statistic.

**Annotated replay collection.** Save short examples of good and poor decisions by theme, with the preceding setup. Build pairs: one overextension and one disciplined pressure play; one late rotation and one timely move. Your own corrected examples make powerful reminders.
